**Viewpoint**

### Regional Transfer Coordination and Hospital Load Balancing During COVID-19 Surges

Steven H. Mitchell, MD; Jessica Rigler, MPH; Karyn Baum, MD, MSEd, MHA

The US health care system relies on highly resourced tertiary and quaternary hospitals receiving patients from hospitals that are unable to provide these levels of specialty care. These latter hospitals, often in sparsely populated rural areas or underresourced urban areas, are typically not equipped to provide the consultative, procedural, and surgical expertise found in larger systems. Substantial effort and expenditure have gone into developing complex systems for transferring patients with time-sensitive conditions, such as trauma, myocardial infarction, and stroke, largely relying on regional centers located in more populated areas.

The recent surge in cases of COVID-19 has reinforced how rapidly hospitals within a broad geographic region can be overwhelmed. Widespread staffing shortages are exacerbating these conditions and substantially constraining health care surge capacity. These problems have been increasingly highlighted in the media as contributors to COVID-19 and non–COVID-19 mortality.<sup>1</sup>

Hospital load-balancing initiatives have been developed in some states to ensure access to necessary inpatient care during the COVID-19 pandemic. As leaders in 3 of these statewide initiatives in Washington, Minnesota, and Arizona, our centers have facilitated the coordination and transfer of more than 15 000 patients from April 2020 through November 2021. In this Viewpoint, we present lessons learned from these centralized, regional, load-balancing services. To promote equitable and timely access to hospital care, we propose that they become a national standard of disaster preparedness when regional demand for specialized hospital care surges.

Statewide coordination centers in all 3 states were created independently in March through May 2020 in response to anticipated surges in patients with COVID-19. Each center began with a common desire to load balance the state’s health care system to prevent crises in overburdened facilities. The centers have worked closely with key collaborators in their health care coalitions, state hospital associations, and state public health departments. Each center uses a statewide bed availability platform to inform decision-making. Funding has been provided through a combination of federal (CARES Act, Hospital Preparedness Program, American Rescue Plan Act) and state public health and emergency funds.

Each coordination center functions according to cooperative and voluntary agreements developed by the state’s key collaborators. For instance, the Washington State Hospital Association developed a letter of shared commitment to load-balancing principles that was signed by every hospital chief executive officer and later a mandated acceptance process with the major health systems once load balancing became more difficult. Arizona's center operates according to a governor’s executive order and has created collaborative protocols with health care systems. The executive order mandates hospital participation with Arizona's center and requires insurers to waive out-of-network rates for care at receiving hospitals when coordinated by Arizona's center.<sup>2</sup> The Minnesota center operates according to a joint agreement between the State Healthcare Coordination Center and the Minnesota Hospital Association.

The 3 centers have varied in their approach to load balancing. The Washington and Minnesota centers coordinate transfers for all patient conditions when existing approaches to interhospital transfers are unsuccessful. Arizona’s center focuses exclusively on patients with COVID-19 whenever a hospital or health system requires transfer. Staffing also varies. The Washington center uses a critical care–trained nurse unaffiliated with a hospital transfer center, Minnesota uses nurses who are incorporated in a regional transfer center, and Arizona employs emergency medical technicians who are trained as transfer agents. Each center is backed by physicians for more complex requests and relies on physician-to-physician clinical decision-making during the transfer process.

Currently, nearly all hospitals with tertiary and high-level critical care services in each of the 3 states are operating near or beyond capacity. Most of these hospitals have been forced to deny or substantially delay transfer requests while they prioritize the patients who are currently in their hospitals and those arriving from their local communities. Many hospitals across the country also have many patients who are boarding in emergency departments or other hospital units that are not normally used for inpatient care, raising concerns about the quality of care and staff burnout. Without a process to coordinate transfers of patients, regardless of where they present, the patients with most critical illness may not obtain care at the sites that are best able to meet their needs.

Of the more than 15 000 transfer requests received by our coordination centers since the pandemic began in March 2020, more than half have originated from rural hospitals serving only about one-tenth of the combined population of the 3 states. As requests from these rural hospitals have swelled in recent months, our coordination centers have been challenged to find appropriate beds in a timely manner. Despite this increased demand, most coordination requests originating from rural hospitals have been successfully placed. Crucially, the Minnesota and Washington centers act as last resorts after other referral options have failed, meaning that those patients might otherwise be left without placement options. The Arizona center functions as the first call for COVID-19 transfers and has received more than 5000 requests from rural hospitals seeking transfers of patients with COVID-19.<sup>3</sup>

Without a centralized means of equitable distribution and access to care, these rural hospitals would need to dedicate staff and resources to calling and pleading for access to care at referral centers (often across multiple states) that are already severely strained and over capacity. Thus, centralized coordination centers provide a mechanism for unaffiliated rural hospitals, transport systems, and postacute care facilities to have patients’ needs addressed along with those of larger health care systems. These coordination centers also provide frontline surveillance data on the health of the health care system, thereby supporting decision-making by hospital leaders and policy makers across broad geographic regions.

In early 2020, few regional coordination centers existed, and as the New York Times reported, “One [New York City] hospital was besieged by the virus. Nearby was ‘plenty of space.’”<sup>4</sup> A recent modeling study suggested that nearly 1 in 4 COVID-19 deaths was “potentially attributable to hospitals strained by surging caseload.”<sup>5</sup> Recently, the National Academy of Medicine recommended that strategies be implemented “to ‘load-balance’ patients and resources regionally to avoid triage decisions, particularly decisions that are likely to lead to adverse and inequitable outcomes.”<sup>6</sup> Bioethicists also have recommended that “governments should ensure that interhospital transfer mechanisms are established and aggressively used to transfer patients from overwhelmed safety net hospitals to better-resourced hospitals.”<sup>7</sup>

The Assistant Secretary for Preparedness and Response has endorsed the creation of Medical Operations Coordination Cells, as exemplified by our centers, to facilitate state and regional load balancing.<sup>8</sup> To our knowledge, a plan to fund and implement this proposal has not been adopted at the federal level. However, several states, including Texas and Nebraska, have incorporated similar centers into their disaster response policies.

We believe that regional, centralized coordination centers are the most ethical, equity-enhancing, feasible, and cost-effective approach to surges in demand for hospital care, whether from a pandemic or a natural disaster. Each of our transfer coordination centers differs in terms of patient eligibility, process, funding, governance, and scope. However, all of our coordination centers have been crucial to providing patients the level of care they need, using scarce critical care resources more effectively and serving disproportionately affected rural populations. Therefore, such centers should be adopted in each state with sustained federal funding as a fundamental component of regional disaster health response systems.
